James' Bolognese Ragu

By James. Reading time: 47 minutes. Cooking time: as long as you can bear.

Jump to recipe

Before we get to the recipe, I need you to understand where this ragu comes from. Not the shop. The soul. To do that, we must travel back in time, to a small city in northern Italy, to a kitchen that smelled of tomatoes and judgement.

My grandmother, my Nonna, grew up in Piacenza. If you do not know Piacenza, it sits on the banks of the river Po, at the very top of Emilia Romagna. Each winter the fog rolls in so thick that people lose their cousins in it. Some of those cousins were never found. The family suspects that they simply moved to Milan, which is worse.

Nonna was the youngest of nine Gazzi children. In the Gazzi house, three things mattered: God, the family and the ragu. Not always in that order. On a Sunday, the priest finished mass early if he could smell the ragu from the church. He could always smell the ragu from the church.

The Gazzi ragu was legendary. People said it could end arguments. People said it could start them. One summer, two farmers who had not spoken for thirty years over a goat shared a bowl of it. They wept, they embraced, and they agreed that the goat had never belonged to either of them. Nobody knows who owns the goat to this day.

The recipe was never written down. It went from mother to daughter, in a whisper, in the kitchen, with the door shut and the radio on loud. My great great grandmother told my great grandmother. My great grandmother told Nonna. The family said that if anyone wrote the recipe down, their wooden spoon would turn to dust in their hand. Nobody tested this. Nobody was brave enough.

As a girl, Nonna's job was to stir. She stirred before school. She stirred after school. The family claims she stirred in her sleep. By the age of twelve, her right arm was so strong that she could open any jar in Piacenza. People came from the villages nearby to ask her to open their jars. She charged one egg per jar, and the Gazzi family never bought an egg again.

The ragu took all day. It started at dawn, when Nonna's mother lit the stove and put the big pot on. It simmered through the morning. It simmered through lunch. It simmered through the long, hot afternoon, while the whole street slept. Nobody lifted the lid without permission. A cousin lifted the lid once without permission. He now lives in Milan.

Then, as a young woman, Nonna left Piacenza for England. She packed one suitcase. It held two dresses, a photograph of her mother and the family pot. The dresses were for emergencies. The pot was not optional.

In England, she found that nobody knew what passata was. At her first visit to the local grocer, she asked for it. He offered her a tin of tomato soup. She looked at the tin. She looked at him. She left without a word and never shopped there again. The family still refers to this as "the soup incident".

My mother grew up on the ragu. Every Sunday, the pot came out, the windows steamed up, and the neighbours found reasons to knock on the door. "We just wondered if you had any sugar," they said. Nobody ever wanted sugar.

And then, one day, it was my turn. I was seven years old. Nonna sat me down at the kitchen table, looked me in the eye and said, "James, one day this recipe will be yours." I waited. She looked at the pot. She looked at me. She looked at the pot again. Then she said, "But not today," and gave me a biscuit.

I asked her every year after that. On every birthday, she said, "Soon." Every Christmas, she said, "Soon." When I passed my driving test, I asked again, and she said, "Drive carefully." That was all. I drove carefully. I did not get the recipe.

Over time, I came to understand that the Gazzi ragu was more than a recipe. It was a promise. It was a thread that ran back through the generations, through the fog of the Po valley, to a kitchen in Piacenza where nine children waited for Sunday. One day, I knew, the whisper would come to me. And I would be ready.

Anyway, I never got the recipe. So one evening I bought a premade bolognese from Kwik Save, ate it, worked out roughly what was in it, and made that instead. Here it is.

Steps

  1. Fry the bacon until just browned. Then add the onion and cook until it is translucent.
  2. Add the mince, season with salt and break it up. Cook until it browns. You do want a bit of pan stickage.
  3. Deglaze the pan with the watered down Worcestershire sauce.
  4. Add the purees, stir them in and cook for a couple of minutes.
  5. Add the passata. Wash out the carton with water (I fill it around half way) and add that water to the pan.
  6. Mix and simmer for as many hours as you possibly can. As it reduces, you will need to add more water from time to time.
  7. About 20 minutes before eating, add the grated parmesan and stir it through.

Important: the Worcestershire sauce and the parmesan are essential for the umami taste.
